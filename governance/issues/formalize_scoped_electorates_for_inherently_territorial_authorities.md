# Formalize Scoped Electorates For Inherently Territorial Authorities

## Summary

The Charter clearly allows Authorities to operate within bounded scopes and clearly provides that renewals are decided by Subscribers within an Authority's scope. It does not yet clearly specify how the electorate for initial formation is determined when the proposed Authority is inherently territorial, local, or service-area dependent.

This is a practical design gap. Without a formal scoped-electorate rule, the system risks either requiring excessively broad voting for small local Authorities or permitting ad hoc local carve-outs without a clear constitutional basis.

## Why This Matters

Some governance functions are inherently geographic in a way that is operational rather than merely political. Fire response, road maintenance, water distribution, emergency medical response, school service areas, and similar functions depend on distance, access, infrastructure, travel time, and physical coverage. They are not naturally organized only by abstract national vote.

If the Charter does not formalize how such scopes are defined and how the relevant electorate is determined at formation, then manageable local governance becomes procedurally uncertain. A requirement that all Subscribers vote on each village fire department would be administratively onerous and inconsistent with the Charter's general commitment to bounded functional scope.

## Current Charter Basis

- Article II allows Authorities to be formed for bounded functional scopes and requires each Authority Charter to define scope.
- Article III recognizes sealed scope and includes territorial fallback for operational conflicts affecting the same geographic area.
- Article VI provides that renewal requires simple-majority approval of Subscribers within the Authority's scope.
- Article XIX recognizes jurisdictions adopting the Charter and incorporating Authorities operating within their scope.

Together, these provisions strongly imply that sub-national or territorially bounded Authorities are compatible with the Charter. The ambiguity is not whether such Authorities may exist. The ambiguity is how their initial electorate is constitutionally identified.

## Problem Statement

The current text does not clearly answer one core formation question:

Who votes to create an Authority whose function is inherently territorial and whose practical service area is much smaller than the full adopting jurisdiction?

Examples include:

- a village or township fire response Authority
- a local water district
- a community road-maintenance Authority
- a school service Authority
- a geographically bounded emergency-response Authority

## Design Goal

Preserve the Charter's anti-expansion and anti-concentration logic while making local Authority formation operationally manageable.

The goal is not to recreate a rigid territorial hierarchy of sovereign units. The goal is to allow territorial service scopes where the function itself requires territory, distance, and physical coverage to be part of the scope definition.

## Likely Direction

One plausible solution is to formalize a rule for inherently territorial Authorities:

- A proposed Authority Charter may define a territorial service scope when the governed function is inherently location-dependent.
- The territorial service scope must be justified using operational criteria relevant to that function.
- Initial formation approval is determined by Subscribers residing within the proposed territorial service scope.
- Renewal continues to be determined by Subscribers within the Authority's scope.
- Overlaps, gaps, and contested boundaries are resolved through Article III scope-conflict rules, inter-Authority compacts, and Article VIII consolidation review where appropriate.

For fire protection, for example, territorial service scope might be justified by response-time targets, road access, terrain, station placement, water access, population density, and incident load rather than by legacy political borders alone.

## Constraints And Risks

- A territorial-scoping rule must not become a loophole for strategic gerrymandering of electorates.
- "Inherently territorial" needs a clearer test so that not every Authority can claim it.
- The rule should distinguish operational territory from general sovereignty. A local fire Authority having a territorial service scope should not imply that it becomes a miniature general government.
- Boundary-setting procedures need a review path when communities dispute inclusion, exclusion, or fragmentation.
- The approach should remain compatible with Article VIII's anti-fragmentation and efficiency concerns.

## Related Questions

- Should the Charter itself define territorial service scope, or should it authorize a later Act or Authority Charter framework to define it?
- Should there be a general constitutional test for when a function is inherently territorial?
- Should a national standards Authority be allowed to define boundary criteria without directly creating subordinate local Authorities?
- What anti-gerrymandering or anti-decoy safeguards are needed for scoped electorates?
- How should the model handle mixed functions that are partly territorial and partly network-based?

## Candidate Follow-On Work

- Draft amendment or clarification language for Article II, Article VI, or Article XVIII.
- Add explainer language describing territorial service scopes for local functions such as fire, water, and roads.
- Test the rule against simulation scenarios involving overlapping service areas and sparse rural populations.
