# Implementation Scenarios

## Purpose

This document collects familiar government functions and frames them as implementation scenarios under the Charter. It is not a binding interpretation. Its purpose is to help readers understand how ordinary civic life could remain recognizably familiar even if the constitutional structure changes.

The working assumption is that most day-to-day public functions would still exist. What changes is not whether those functions exist, but how they are chartered, scoped, reviewed, and constrained.

## Everyday-Life Scenarios To Flesh Out

- Local police functions that people ordinarily encounter
- Water management authority
- Fire department
- National-scale FBI-style crime fighting
- Public schools and school districts
- Courts for ordinary civil disputes
- Public health departments
- Roads, transit, and street maintenance
- Utilities regulation and service continuity
- Emergency management and disaster response
- Property records, zoning, and building permits
- Elections administration
- Licensing and inspections
- Corrections and rehabilitation
- Consumer and labor protections
- Environmental protection beyond water

## Proposal Framing

The proposals below are not intended to pre-write the final Authority Charters. They are working sketches showing how familiar functions would likely be implemented inside the Charter's structure. In each case, the key constraints are the same: sealed scope under Articles II and III, narrow interpretation under Article X, periodic review under Article VI, cross-Authority coordination through compacts and ECO under Articles III and XIII, and rights review through the Charter Court under Articles XII, XVI, and XVII.

In practice, that means ordinary public services could still exist and still feel familiar to most people. The difference is that each service would need a clearer constitutional justification, a narrower operational charter, and stronger public logging and review than many present-day institutions face.

## Local Police Functions That People Ordinarily Encounter

Local police functions would likely be handled by one or more coercive Public Safety Authorities chartered at the municipal or regional level. Because enforcement and adjudication-related powers are coercive under Article II, creation would require the higher seventy-five percent approval threshold. Their sealed scope would likely include patrol response, immediate threat interdiction, arrest authority defined by Legislative Act, evidence preservation, traffic incident response, and cooperation with Authority courts and the Charter Court.

Under the Charter, the most important change is that local police would not be treated as holders of a general police power. Their Authority Charter would need to specify exactly which categories of conduct they may enforce, how they may detain, search, transport, and document, and what data they may collect and retain. Article X's Minimal Necessary Power rule would cut against broad implied discretion. Article XVII's transparent enforcement requirement and Article XI's due process protections would push toward visible procedures, public logging of policies, and reviewable use-of-force and detention rules.

This would likely also separate functions that are often fused today. Routine patrol, detention management, forensic services, and prosecutorial support may need to be split among distinct Authorities or substructures if combining them creates too much coercive concentration. Cross-Authority enforcement at the constitutional level would remain unavailable to them, because CAES exists only to execute Charter Court orders. So everyday policing remains familiar in the street-level sense, but it becomes more modular, more procedurally explicit, and less able to grow by custom or administrative habit.

## Water Management Authority

Water management is a strong example of a non-coercive Authority with possible coercive edges. A Water Management Authority could be chartered to manage reservoirs, treatment systems, aquifer protection, drainage networks, flood-control infrastructure, usage planning, and interjurisdictional water-sharing compacts. If its role remained primarily operational and administrative, Article II suggests it could be formed at the sixty percent threshold as a non-coercive Authority. If it also exercised binding regulatory or sanctioning powers over private users, that portion would likely require coercive treatment or a separate linked Authority.

The Charter would encourage a clean separation between infrastructure management and punitive enforcement. The water Authority could monitor supply, publish usage conditions, maintain capital plans, and coordinate drought or contamination responses with ECO during emergencies. If there were a need for fines, shutoff orders, compulsory access, or property restrictions, those powers should be tightly enumerated and likely either separately chartered or expressly limited as coercive exceptions. That reduces the tendency for technical utilities to drift into general land-use or public-order power.

This scenario also illustrates Article III's inter-Authority compact model. Water systems routinely cross local boundaries, so explicit one-year renewable compacts would likely be common. In a flood or contamination emergency, a water Authority might become the Lead Emergency Authority if it has the most direct functional jurisdiction over the precipitating threat. Even then, emergency powers would remain time-limited, publicly posted, and reviewable by the Charter Court.

## Fire Department

Fire protection would likely sit inside a Fire Response Authority with a mostly non-coercive core and narrowly defined emergency coercive powers. Its ordinary scope would include fire suppression, rescue operations, hazardous-material incident response, fire prevention inspections if separately authorized, and emergency medical first response where locally assigned. Because the central function is immediate life and property protection rather than general regulation, this could plausibly be chartered as a non-coercive Authority unless inspection and enforcement powers were substantial enough to require a coercive charter.

The Charter structure would push fire response toward mission clarity. A fire Authority would be able to enter dangerous scenes, evacuate structures during active emergencies, secure hazardous perimeters, and coordinate mutual aid, but it would not thereby gain broad investigative or policing authority. After-action investigation into criminal arson, for example, would likely require coordination with a criminal investigation Authority rather than unilateral expansion of fire powers.

Article V is especially important here. Fire and related rescue bodies are among the clearest candidates for provisional emergency activation under the seventy-two-hour rule, because they often must act immediately to prevent loss of life or catastrophic infrastructure failure. That power would exist, but it would still be bounded. Immediate action does not convert the fire Authority into a general emergency government, and emergency measures would expire unless validated under the Charter's normal procedures.

## National-Scale FBI-Style Crime Fighting

A national investigative function would likely exist, but it would need a much narrower constitutional design than a modern all-purpose federal investigative bureau. The most plausible structure is a coercive National Serious Crimes Authority with sealed jurisdiction over specified classes of cross-regional, organized, systemic, or national-security-adjacent crime that are still domestic and civilian in nature. Because the Charter sharply separates defense from domestic enforcement in Articles XIII and XIV, this body could not simply blend external intelligence, military capability, and civil investigation into one apparatus.

Its charter would need to enumerate triggering predicates for jurisdiction: multijurisdictional organized crime, public corruption, major financial fraud above a threshold, coordinated cybercrime, trafficking networks, terrorism or violent conspiracy within the civil sphere, and other specifically defined offenses. It would not receive residual authority over any case that seems important. Local and sectoral Authorities would retain primary competence unless the national investigative charter clearly attaches. That makes national-scale crime fighting possible while limiting the institutional habit of self-expansion.

The Charter would also reshape oversight. Warrants, process, and disputes would run through competent Authority courts with Charter Court review for rights or cross-Authority conflicts. Public transparency rules would require a much more visible legal basis for surveillance, data retention, and inter-Authority information sharing, even if operational details remain protected where privacy and active investigations require it. The result is an FBI-style function that still exists, but as a chartered instrument for a narrow class of serious, cross-boundary crimes rather than as a general national domestic-intelligence center.

## Public Schools And School Districts

Education would likely be administered through non-coercive Education Authorities organized at local or regional scale, with possible separate curriculum, credentialing, and funding structures depending on how much decentralization the jurisdiction wants. Their core scope would include operating schools, hiring staff, maintaining facilities, publishing standards, administering student support services, and managing school transportation if assigned. Because the basic function is service provision rather than coercion, Article II suggests formation at the lower threshold unless disciplinary or regulatory powers are expanded beyond ordinary institutional administration.

Under the Charter, school governance would likely become clearer about what counts as educational function and what does not. A school Authority could teach, assess, transport, feed, and support students, but it would not automatically acquire broad family-policing, mental-health detention, or generalized civic-monitoring powers. Student discipline would still exist, but it would have to conform to due process and rights protections, with coercive measures narrowly justified and reviewable. That is a meaningful change from systems where school administration often accumulates quasi-policing powers without a clean constitutional basis.

The modular model also allows different education functions to be distributed. One Authority might operate schools, another certify teachers through ACB-compatible standards, and another manage fiscal distribution through Treasury-linked Funding Acts. To ordinary families, daily life could still look like enrollment, attendance zones or choice frameworks, buses, teachers, and report cards. The constitutional difference is that education remains a service Authority with bounded powers rather than a broad social-governance platform.

## Courts For Ordinary Civil Disputes

Ordinary civil disputes would likely be handled by Authority courts attached to one or more Civil Adjudication Authorities or by internal courts within function-specific Authorities, depending on how broadly the system chooses to organize civil justice. Small claims, landlord-tenant disputes, contract enforcement, probate, family matters, and similar questions are plainly adjudicative and therefore coercive under Article II. Their institutions would require the higher approval threshold and carefully specified jurisdictional boundaries.

The Charter already anticipates internal Authority courts in Article XII. That suggests a layered model in which some disputes are heard inside the Authority most directly connected to the underlying function, while appeals reach the Charter Court only on rights or cross-Authority grounds. A housing-related Authority could have its own tribunal for routine housing disputes; a more general civil court structure could hear matters that do not fall inside a specialized scope. The key design constraint is that no civil court should silently evolve into a general lawmaking body or a catch-all social regulator.

For daily life, this still looks familiar: people file claims, receive hearings, present evidence, get judgments, and seek redress before impartial tribunals. The structural difference is that civil adjudication exists as a bounded service with explicit jurisdiction and clearer appellate limits. CAES would execute Charter Court orders, but routine execution mechanisms for judgments would have to be chartered within lawful civil-enforcement processes rather than implied as part of an unlimited judicial apparatus.

## Public Health Departments

A Public Health Authority would likely be one of the clearest service-oriented Authorities under the Charter. Its ordinary scope could include disease surveillance, vaccination logistics, community health campaigns, laboratory coordination, sanitation guidance, restaurant inspection if assigned, and outbreak response planning. Depending on how inspection and closure powers are structured, the Authority may need either a mixed charter or a companion coercive enforcement body for sanctions and compulsory orders.

The Charter would particularly matter in boundary-setting. Public health often drifts between technical guidance, service provision, and coercive intervention. Under this system, a health Authority could gather epidemiological information, issue advisories, coordinate hospitals, and recommend mitigation measures without automatically gaining open-ended power over movement, business operation, or bodily autonomy. Any coercive measures would need explicit legal grounding, narrow tailoring, due process, and compatibility with Articles XVI and XVII.

Emergency powers remain available but not self-justifying. In a serious outbreak, a health Authority might become the Lead Emergency Authority if the threat clearly falls within its functional jurisdiction. But emergency status would not erase rights review. The Charter Court could still evaluate whether quarantine rules, data collection, or movement restrictions are genuinely necessary, time-limited, and compatible with unalienable and procedural rights. That makes public health response possible without treating expertise as a standing exception to constitutional limits.

## Roads, Transit, And Street Maintenance

These functions fit naturally within one or more non-coercive Infrastructure and Transit Authorities. Their scope would likely include road maintenance, signal systems, bridge upkeep, snow removal, public transit operations, route planning, and capital scheduling for transport assets. Because these are ordinary public-service functions, they would generally sit on the non-coercive side unless paired with sanctioning, compulsory acquisition, or enforcement powers beyond narrow operational needs.

The Charter's modular structure would likely separate ownership and operation questions from policing and adjudication. A transit Authority could run buses and trains, publish schedules, maintain vehicles, and coordinate with road-maintenance bodies, but fare enforcement, compulsory removals, or major land-use takings would need specifically chartered authority. This distinction is useful because transportation agencies in many systems gradually absorb quasi-regulatory powers that are only loosely supervised.

To most residents, daily life would feel largely unchanged: buses arrive, roads get repaired, signals are timed, and crews clear snow. The difference is constitutional housekeeping. Inter-Authority compacts would govern boundary crossings and shared corridors, Treasury-linked funding would be visible on public ledgers, and performance review under Article VI would force periodic justification of major transport institutions rather than leaving them to accumulate indefinitely.

## Utilities Regulation And Service Continuity

Utility continuity is likely to require at least two distinct functions under the Charter: an operational service Authority for public utility systems where the jurisdiction owns or directly manages them, and a coercive regulatory Authority where private or mixed providers must be compelled to meet public obligations. Electricity, gas, sewer, and broadband all involve ongoing service standards, infrastructure reliability, and emergency restoration, but those are not all the same constitutional power.

The Charter would discourage building one giant utility super-agency that both owns systems, writes standards, adjudicates disputes, and punishes violations. A more compliant design would split technical planning and service continuity from enforcement and adjudication. The regulatory side would need the higher approval threshold because rate orders, compliance mandates, and sanctions are coercive. The operational side could remain non-coercive if it primarily manages assets and service delivery.

This arrangement keeps ordinary life familiar while making institutional power more legible. People still expect electricity to stay on, sewage to work, and outages to be restored quickly. Under the Charter, those expectations remain, but the legal basis for continuity mandates, emergency prioritization, customer remedies, and provider penalties would have to be explicit rather than buried inside broad administrative custom.

## Emergency Management And Disaster Response

Emergency management is one of the most important functions to explain because the Charter expressly allows emergency power but tries to keep it fenced in. A Disaster Response Authority or Emergency Management Authority could be formed to plan evacuation systems, shelters, continuity operations, logistics staging, inter-Authority communications, and post-disaster coordination. Its ordinary role would likely be mostly non-coercive and heavily collaborative with ECO.

The emergency challenge is that coordination bodies often expand into de facto executive governments. The Charter resists that outcome. ECO may coordinate logistics but may not declare emergencies or command coercive force. A dedicated emergency-management Authority may plan and organize, but coercive emergency actions must still be tied to Article V, functional jurisdiction, time limits, and Charter Court review. That structure permits speed without creating a permanent emergency sovereign.

For the public, this still means familiar systems: alerts, shelters, evacuation routes, supply staging, and restoration planning. What changes is the legal architecture. The Authority that plans for disaster does not automatically own every tool used during disaster, and temporary dominance under the territorial fallback rule does not expand permanent scope. Once the emergency ends, the Authority contracts back to its normal charter.

## Property Records, Zoning, And Building Permits

This category would probably divide into at least two Authorities. Property records administration is largely a non-coercive registry function: deed recording, title indexing, map maintenance, and public access to land records. Zoning and permitting, by contrast, are regulatory and adjudicative functions that directly condition private conduct and therefore likely require coercive chartering.

The Charter would push these institutions toward much clearer boundary lines. A Property Registry Authority could maintain authoritative records and transactional filing systems without gaining policy discretion over land use. A Land Use and Building Authority could issue permits, apply building and safety standards, and manage hearings on compliance or variances if those powers are expressly granted. The two may coordinate, but Article III would resist letting the recordkeeping body slowly become a planning and enforcement body merely because it holds the data.

This is a good example of everyday continuity with constitutional disaggregation. Residents and builders would still apply for permits, record deeds, check parcel maps, and undergo inspections. The difference is that each step would rest on a more visible institutional basis, with fewer implied powers and better routes for challenging scope overreach or rights violations.

## Representation Registry And Vote Administration

This Charter does not primarily rely on recurring public elections to choose and replace Representatives. Subscribers instead delegate representation tokens, can change those delegations over time, and have those changes activate on a delayed schedule under Article VII. The implementation scenario to explain, then, is not a conventional election commission but a representation-registry and vote-administration function that keeps the delegation system legible, auditable, and procedurally reliable.

Its probable scope would include subscriber-roll maintenance, delegation submission intake, public logging of delegation changes, activation-date tracking, stabilization-window administration when large numbers of shifts are pending, concentration-cap auditing, petition-signature verification, representative roster maintenance, and legislative vote-record publication. For legislative business, it would likely also run scheduled vote windows or deadlines by which Representatives must register support, opposition, or abstention under whatever procedural rules valid Legislative Acts establish. Once the deadline closes, the matter would either pass at the applicable threshold and receive the duration attached to that approval level under Article IV, or it would drop from consideration.

The Charter would also place hard limits on what this administrative body may do. It could maintain records, publish deadlines, authenticate submissions, and certify whether delegation or voting procedures were followed, but it would not become a general speech regulator, party manager, or public-order body. Nor should it gain open-ended discretion to decide who may politically organize, which viewpoints may circulate, or which proposals deserve consideration beyond the lawful procedural rules. Because representation changes, petition support, and legislative vote registration all affect constitutional legitimacy, public logging and review would be especially important here.

For ordinary people, the experience would be less like standing in line for frequent elections and more like maintaining an ongoing civic account relationship. A Subscriber checks the public registry, delegates to a Representative, changes that delegation when desired subject to timing limits, and watches legislative matters move through public vote deadlines. The administrative role remains familiar in one sense because someone still has to maintain records and certify procedure, but the core task is managing a rolling delegation-and-vote system rather than staging periodic winner-take-all elections.

## Licensing And Inspections

Licensing and inspections would almost certainly be distributed across multiple Authorities rather than consolidated into one universal office. Driver licensing, professional licensing, business permits, food-service inspections, vehicle registration, and construction inspections involve different functional scopes and different risk profiles. The Charter's anti-fragmentation rule would block artificial slicing for political convenience, but the sealed-scope rule would also block over-centralization into a single omnibus regulator unless that breadth were clearly justified and approved.

Most licensing regimes involve coercive power because they determine whether people may lawfully engage in regulated activities. That means explicit standards, appeals, evidentiary procedures, and transparent criteria become central. Article XVIII's ACB structure is also relevant here. In some fields, competence certification may belong partly to independent certification bodies rather than entirely to the operational Authority that uses the credential.

Everyday life would still involve getting a license, renewing it, scheduling an inspection, or appealing a denial. The difference is that each licensing system would need a more precise charter and a narrower reason for existence. That should make it easier to distinguish legitimate safety regulation from institutional convenience or covert gatekeeping.

## Corrections And Rehabilitation

Corrections is among the most constitutionally sensitive ordinary functions because it sits at the intersection of coercion, due process, and human dignity. A Corrections Authority could exist to manage detention facilities, sentence administration, probation structures, parole systems if authorized, and rehabilitation services. Because it plainly exercises coercive power, it would require the higher creation threshold and unusually tight rights protections.

Under the Charter, corrections should not be treated as a floating residual power attached to police or courts. Its scope would have to be expressly chartered, and Article XVI's rights to bodily autonomy, freedom from coercion except under due process, and equitable treatment would sharply constrain facility conditions, classification practices, surveillance, and sanctions. Article XVII's right to seek redress before impartial tribunals would also support formal grievance and review mechanisms rather than purely internal discipline.

The rehabilitation side is equally important. If the Charter intends to resist domination rather than merely rename state force, a Corrections Authority should likely be required to distinguish incapacitation, accountability, and reintegration functions instead of collapsing them into one punitive model. Daily life still includes prisons, jails, supervision, and release processes, but the institutional justification for each step becomes narrower and more reviewable.

## Consumer And Labor Protections

Consumer and labor protection could be implemented through one or more coercive Market Fairness Authorities with sealed jurisdiction over deceptive practices, wage claims, workplace safety, collective-action protections, and standardized complaint processes. These are coercive because they impose legal duties, investigate violations, and may issue sanctions or compliance orders. Their charters would therefore need to be explicit about subject matter and remedy types.

The Charter would likely improve clarity here by preventing a general economic-morality mandate. A labor-protection Authority could enforce wage and safety rules, run claims systems, and inspect relevant workplaces if authorized, but it would not thereby gain broad control over corporate organization, political speech, or industrial planning. A consumer-protection Authority could police fraud and unfair practices within defined categories without becoming an all-purpose commerce ministry.

For ordinary residents, the familiar functions remain recognizable: filing a wage complaint, reporting a scam, seeking workplace inspection, or obtaining a remedy for deceptive trade conduct. The Charter's contribution is structural discipline. These protections continue, but they continue inside defined legal boxes with stronger transparency, review, and renewal requirements.

## Environmental Protection Beyond Water

Environmental protection outside water would likely require either a general Environmental Stewardship Authority with tightly stated powers or several functionally distinct Authorities for air, waste, habitat, hazardous substances, and remediation. The right design depends on whether those functions are genuinely integrated or merely administratively adjacent. Article VIII's anti-fragmentation and efficiency concerns argue against needless multiplication, but Articles II and III argue just as strongly against vague ecological super-authorities.

A practical model would give the environmental Authority non-coercive monitoring, science, and remediation-planning functions while pairing regulatory and sanctioning powers with explicit coercive authorization. Air-quality standards, hazardous-spill orders, landfill restrictions, and remediation mandates all involve compelled conduct and would need clear statutory grounding. The Charter would press hard against using environmental language as a catch-all rationale for indefinite intervention in unrelated domains.

This still leaves ample room for familiar everyday functions: emissions oversight, hazardous-waste response, public reporting, conservation management, and cleanup obligations. What changes is the constitutional expectation that environmental administration must stay inside its enumerated purpose, coordinate through explicit compacts when it touches infrastructure or health, and remain open to review when rights, property, or scope boundaries are disputed.

## Why This Matters

Many readers evaluate a charter by asking a practical question: what happens to the public systems people already depend on? These proposals show one likely answer. Daily life would still contain patrol officers, teachers, firefighters, road crews, inspectors, courts, and utility managers. The Charter does not eliminate those familiar functions.

What it changes is the constitutional posture of those functions. Each one must be created for a defined job, limited to that job, reviewed over time, and prevented from silently expanding into a general-purpose institution. That is the central implementation implication of Jefferson Mark III: ordinary governance remains, but it remains on a shorter constitutional leash.
