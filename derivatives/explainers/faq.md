# FAQ

## What is the Charter trying to build?

A governance system where power starts with Subscribers, can be delegated, and can only be exercised through Authorities that have clearly defined functions, public oversight, and limited duration.

## Who holds sovereignty?

Subscribers do. The Charter explicitly places sovereignty in Subscribers rather than in an office, state apparatus, or permanent governing body.

## What is a Subscriber?

A Subscriber is the base political member of the system. Each Subscriber gets one representation token and can participate in petitioning, delegation, and governance.

## What is a Representative?

A Representative is any Subscriber who receives delegation tokens from other Subscribers.

## What is an Authority?

An Authority is a governance body created under the Charter for a specific functional scope. It is not supposed to be general-purpose. It can act only inside the powers explicitly granted to it.

## Why are some Authorities harder to create than others?

Because the Charter treats coercive power as more dangerous than non-coercive coordination. Authorities with coercive powers need 75% approval. Non-coercive Authorities need 60% approval.

## Can an Authority expand its own power?

No. Scope expansions require rechartering under the same approval thresholds that applied to creation.

## How does delegation work?

Each Subscriber can delegate their one representation token to any Representative. Changes take effect after thirty days, are publicly logged, and are limited in frequency.

## Why is delegation weighted instead of purely linear?

The Charter tries to prevent influence from scaling too easily into domination. Larger blocs still matter, but marginal gains diminish as delegations accumulate.

## Why is there a cap on representation?

To prevent political concentration. No Representative may hold more than one percent of total raw delegation tokens, and affiliated coalitions can be treated as one Representative for cap purposes.

## How do laws pass?

Legislative Acts normally pass by simple majority. Their duration then depends on how much support they received, with broader support producing longer-lasting Acts.

## What happens when laws conflict?

The Charter first prefers the Act with the stronger approval majority. If support is equal, the narrower functional scope prevails within that scope. If the conflict still cannot be resolved, the Charter Court may issue a temporary harmonization order.

## What happens in an emergency?

A Lead Emergency Authority can act for thirty days in a qualifying emergency. Extensions require additional approval, emergency measures must be publicly posted, and the Charter Court can review them for rights compliance.

## Can emergency power become permanent?

Not automatically. Emergency powers expire with the emergency, and extensions are structured and reviewable.

## What protects individual rights?

The Charter includes due process, transparency, privacy, participation rights, freedom of thought and expression, bodily autonomy, protection against coercion outside due process, freedom of assembly, and freedom of movement.

## Who interprets the Charter?

The Charter Court does. It handles constitutional interpretation, rights issues, and Authority scope conflicts.

## What is CAES?

CAES is the Cross-Authority Enforcement Service. It exists to execute Charter Court orders and is not allowed to initiate policy or independent enforcement action.

## What is ECO?

The Executive Coordination Office handles cross-Authority coordination, logistics, and administration. It may coordinate emergencies, but it may not declare them and may not command coercive force.

## Why does the Charter use random selection so often?

Sortition is used to reduce entrenchment, lower the value of capturing fixed offices, and create more independent oversight bodies.

## What is the point of consolidation review?

The Charter expects institutions to multiply, overlap, and sometimes become inefficient or strategic. Consolidation review is the system's way of pruning unnecessary or manipulative Authority structures.

## Is the Charter the authoritative source?

Yes. `charter/charter.md` is the canonical text. Explainers, annotations, and simulation files must be kept consistent with it.

## Is the PDF authoritative?

No. The PDF is only a formatted export unless the project later states otherwise.
