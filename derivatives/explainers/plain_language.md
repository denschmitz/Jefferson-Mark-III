# Plain Language Explainer

## What This Charter Is Trying To Do

This Charter is a blueprint for a political system that tries to solve a familiar problem: how do you coordinate large groups of people without letting power become permanent, unaccountable, or too concentrated?

Its answer is to make political authority conditional. Power does not belong to offices by default. It begins with Subscribers, who can delegate their representation to Representatives, create Authorities for specific purposes, and withdraw support over time. Authority is allowed, but only inside clearly defined limits.

## The Big Idea In Simple Terms

This system is built around five basic ideas.

1. People remain the source of authority.
2. Government bodies can only exist for clearly defined jobs.
3. Bigger or more coercive powers require stronger approval.
4. Most powers expire, renew, or face review instead of lasting forever.
5. Rights and transparency rules sit above ordinary governance.

## How Representation Works

Every Subscriber gets one representation token. They can delegate it to any Representative. That delegation is voluntary and reversible, but it does not take effect instantly. Changes activate after thirty days, and there are limits on how often people can switch. So representation can change over time without waiting for a fixed election cycle.

The Charter also tries to prevent popularity from turning into domination. Delegation is weighted using a concave formula, which means bigger coalitions still gain influence, but not in a straight line. The system also caps how much representation any one Representative can hold and treats coordinated coalitions as one person for cap purposes.

In plain English: people can gather support, but the system is designed to slow the emergence of political super-actors.

## How Government Bodies Are Created

The Charter calls governing bodies Authorities. An Authority is not supposed to be a general-purpose government department that keeps expanding forever. It is supposed to have a sealed functional scope.

That means:

- it must be created for a defined purpose;
- it must publish its own Authority Charter;
- it cannot assume powers that were not explicitly granted;
- if it wants more power, it must go back through the approval process.

The approval threshold depends on the seriousness of the power involved.

- Coercive Authorities need 75% approval.
- Non-coercive Authorities need 60% approval.

This is the Charter's way of saying that force, regulation, and adjudication need broader public consent than coordination or administration.

## How Laws Work

Legislative Acts usually pass by simple majority. In practice, that means a proposal survives if the relevant vote clears the threshold before the decision window closes. The Charter then adds an unusual rule: stronger approval gives a longer lifespan.

- 51-54% support gives a law one year.
- 55-64% gives three years.
- 65-74% gives six years.
- 75% or more gives ten years.

So the system does not treat every majority as equally durable. Narrow majorities can act, but they get shorter-lasting outcomes. Broader agreement buys more stability.

## What Happens In Emergencies

The Charter allows emergency powers, but it tries to keep them fenced in.

A Lead Emergency Authority can act for thirty days when there is a qualifying emergency. Extensions require wider approval, and very long emergencies eventually require a National Decision. Emergency measures must be posted publicly, and the Charter Court can review them against rights protections.

There is also a short provisional emergency power for immediate threats, but it lasts only seventy-two hours unless validated through the normal process.

The basic principle is simple: emergencies can justify speed, but not unlimited power.

## How The System Checks Itself

The Charter uses several overlapping control mechanisms.

- Authorities have sealed scope.
- Authorities undergo periodic review and renewal.
- Low satisfaction can trigger mandatory reauthorization review.
- Consolidation reviews examine overlap and redundancy.
- Courts review rights violations and scope conflicts.
- Public logging is required across major parts of the system.

This means the system does not rely on one single safeguard. It uses many smaller safeguards layered together.

## What Rights People Keep

The Charter protects due process, participation, transparency, privacy, freedom of thought, freedom of expression, freedom of movement, freedom of assembly, and protection against coercion outside due process.

It also explicitly protects people from being forced to delegate or politically associate. That matters because the whole governance model relies on representation and delegation. The Charter is trying to make participation available without making conformity mandatory.

## What Makes This Charter Different

Compared with a familiar nation-state constitution, this Charter is unusual in a few ways.

- It treats governance as modular instead of centralized.
- It assumes institutions should expire, renew, or justify themselves repeatedly.
- It relies heavily on sortition, using random selection for councils, courts, assemblies, and oversight bodies.
- It tries to separate coordination from coercion.
- It treats concentration of representation as a structural risk.

## What An Average Reader Should Take Away

The Charter is trying to build a government that is strong enough to coordinate but weak enough to stay accountable. It does that by dividing functions, limiting scope, requiring transparency, protecting rights, and making consent an ongoing condition rather than a one-time founding event.

If the system works as intended, no office rules by default, no mandate lasts forever, and every important power remains answerable to the people who authorized it.
